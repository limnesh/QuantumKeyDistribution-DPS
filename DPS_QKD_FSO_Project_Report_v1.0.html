Abstract¶↑ Contents

This report develops a numerical study of differential phase-shift quantum key distribution (DPS-QKD) over free-space optical (FSO) links. It begins with post-quantum confidentiality needs, quantum/classical communication and authentication, then compares five protocol families: BB84, BBM92, Ekert91 (E91), Coherent One-Way (COW) and DPS. The reconciliation chapter explains Cascade, Winnow, LDPC and polar-code methods, together with key verification and Toeplitz privacy amplification. The main simulation then evaluates DPS pulse interference, weak coherent photon statistics, an illustrative modeled secret-key-rate (SKR) proxy, 20 km terrestrial optics, log-normal turbulence, detection-weighted QBER, LEO downlinks and trusted satellite networks.

The study further investigates the application of machine learning to optimize DPS-QKD performance by comparing conventional physics-based optimization with an ML-assisted approach. The results demonstrate the potential of machine learning to identify improved operating parameters and enhance the modeled secret key rate, while highlighting the trade-offs between optimization performance and computational efficiency. The simulation is also implemented in GNU Octave for comparative analysis and interactive visualization.

Introduction and Problem Statement¶↑ Contents

This section introduces the long-term confidentiality challenges posed by advances in quantum computing and their implications for existing cryptographic systems. It discusses emerging quantum security threats and introduces Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) and Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) as complementary approaches to securing future communications.

Long-Term Confidentiality and the Quantum Threat¶↑ Contents

Modern digital communication relies heavily on cryptographic algorithms to protect sensitive information transmitted across public and private networks. Although current encryption systems provide strong protection against conventional computing attacks, many widely deployed security protocols depend on public-key algorithms such as RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) for key establishment and authentication. The rapid advancement of quantum computing introduces a significant long-term security concern because a sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer could break these algorithms using techniques such as Shor’s algorithm. This creates the Harvest-Now, Decrypt-Later (HNDL) threat, in which adversaries intercept and store encrypted communications today with the intention of decrypting them when future quantum computing capabilities become available. Information requiring confidentiality for many years, including financial records, healthcare data, government communications, intellectual property and critical infrastructure information, is particularly exposed to this risk. Although strong symmetric encryption such as AES-256 is not immediately considered broken by the same quantum attack, the compromise of vulnerable key-establishment mechanisms could expose the keys used to protect recorded communications.

Shor’s Algorithm¶↑ Contents

RSA relies on a problem that is easy to perform in one direction and computationally difficult to reverse: multiplying two very large prime numbers is straightforward, while recovering those factors from their product is impractical for current conventional computers at cryptographic sizes. Shor’s algorithm changes this security assumption because a sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer could factor large integers efficiently. NIST notes that factoring sufficiently large integers could take billions of years using conventional computers, whereas a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could potentially complete the same task in hours or days. Current quantum computers are not yet capable of breaking RSA-2048.

Quantum-Safe Approaches: PQC and QKD¶↑ Contents

This problem motivates the investigation of quantum-resistant approaches, including post-quantum cryptography and Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), which uses quantum-mechanical principles to establish shared secret keys. When appropriately implemented alongside authenticated communication, secure key management and strong symmetric encryption, QKD offers an alternative key-distribution mechanism for protecting sensitive information against future cryptographic threats.

Example: Suppose an organization has data that must remain confidential for 10 years. An attacker records encrypted traffic today. If the session keys were established using a public-key method that is vulnerable to a future quantum computer, and a sufficiently capable quantum-computing service becomes available before the 10-year confidentiality period expires, the attacker could potentially recover the old session secrets and decrypt the stored traffic while the information is still sensitive.

Possible organizational impact includes:

  • Exposure of confidential customer, financial, technical, or business data;

  • Breach of contractual or regulatory confidentiality obligations;

  • Loss of intellectual property or competitive information;

  • Incident-response, legal, notification, and recovery costs;

  • Reputational damage even though the original traffic was captured years earlier.

The purpose of quantum-safe key establishment is therefore not only to protect communications against attacks available today, but also to protect information whose required secrecy lifetime extends into the period when large fault-tolerant quantum computers may exist.

Fundamentals of Quantum Key Distribution¶↑ Contents

This section introduces the communication model common to the QKD protocols used in this study. It distinguishes the quantum channel, the authenticated classical channel and the classical post-processing stages that transform raw detection events into shared key material.

Quantum and Classical Channels, Threats and Authentication¶↑ Contents

Depending on the protocol, Alice prepares quantum signals for Bob (for example BB84/DPS) or Alice and Bob measure signals distributed from an entangled source (for example BBM92). They use an authenticated classical channel for the required public discussion—such as basis information, detection positions, parameter estimation, reconciliation data and verification—without publishing the undisclosed raw key. They estimate errors, reconcile, verify equality, and compress the key to limit Eve's information. Eve may observe or manipulate the optical channel and public discussion; authentication blocks an otherwise fatal man-in-the-middle impersonation. Photon loss or errors alone cannot identify Eve: hardware imperfections also cause them. Real devices require security analysis against side channels.

Example of why authentication matters: Without it, Eve can separately negotiate a key with Alice and another with Bob, then read and forward messages. A QBER check in each forged session need not expose her, because Eve is acting as an endpoint of two separate sessions rather than merely measuring and resending individual signals.

Why QKD Still Requires Authentication¶↑ Contents

QKD can reveal disturbances under its protocol and device assumptions, but it does not automatically prove the identity of the communicating parties.

The classical channel used for:

  • Basis announcements,

  • Parameter estimation,

  • Syndrome exchange,

  • Error correction, and

  • Privacy-amplification coordination

must therefore be authenticated.

If the classical channel is not authenticated, Eve can perform a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack.

Instead of allowing Alice and Bob to establish one common key,

Alice↔BobAlice \longleftrightarrow Bob

Eve can create two independent connections:

Alice↔EveAlice \longleftrightarrow Eve

and

Eve↔BobEve \longleftrightarrow Bob

Alice may believe that her key is shared with Bob, while it is actually shared with Eve.
Bob may similarly believe that his key is shared with Alice, while it is actually shared with Eve.

Therefore, matching keys within a QKD session do not by themselves prove the identity of the remote party.

Worked authentication example

Eve negotiates two separate keys. Each forged session passes a local equality check, yet Alice and Bob do not share the same key. This example separates identity authentication from error estimation on an optical link.

QKD Key-Processing Flow¶↑ Contents

At a high level, QKD proceeds from quantum-state preparation or entangled-state measurement to valid-event selection, parameter estimation and QBER sampling, information reconciliation, key verification and privacy amplification. These steps are coordinated over an authenticated classical channel without publishing the undisclosed raw key; the resulting shared key can then be supplied to conventional cryptographic protection.

BB84 Protocol Demonstration¶↑ Contents

BB84 encodes each classical bit using one of two randomly selected quantum bases.

Two BB84 bases

The rectilinear basis (Z basis) uses:

  • Bit 0 -> Horizontal polarization, H

  • Bit 1 -> Vertical polarization, V

The diagonal basis (X basis) uses:

  • Bit 0 -> +45 degree polarization, D

  • Bit 1 -> -45 degree polarization, A

In this simulation we represent the bases numerically as:

  • 0 -> Z basis (H/V)

  • 1 -> X basis (D/A)

Alice randomly generates:

  1. A raw binary key.

  2. A random basis for every bit.

  3. The corresponding quantum state.

Bob does not know Alice's basis while measuring the incoming photons. He therefore independently chooses a random measurement basis for every photon.

If Bob chooses the same basis as Alice, he obtains Alice's bit correctly in this idealized simulation.

If Bob chooses the wrong basis, quantum mechanics gives a random result, so Bob has a 50% probability of obtaining either 0 or 1.

After the quantum transmission is complete, Alice and Bob publicly announce only their basis choices over an authenticated classical channel.

They do not reveal their secret bit values.

They keep only positions where their bases matched. This process is called basis sifting.

For random independent basis choices,

P(basis match)=12P(\text{basis~match}) = \frac{1}{2}

so approximately half of the transmitted bits survive BB84 sifting.

BBM92 Protocol Demonstration¶↑ Contents

BBM92 is an entanglement-based quantum key distribution protocol.

Unlike BB84, Alice does not prepare a classical bit and encode it into a photon.

Instead, an entangled-photon source generates pairs of photons. One photon is sent to Alice and the other photon is sent to Bob.

In this educational example, the source produces the Bell state:

Φ+⟩=HH⟩+VV⟩2\Phi^{+ \rangle = \frac{HH\rangle + VV\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}}

The same state can also be written in the diagonal basis as:

Φ+⟩=DD⟩+AA⟩2\Phi^{+ \rangle = \frac{DD\rangle + AA\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}}

Therefore, if Alice and Bob measure their photons using the same basis, their measurement results are perfectly correlated in an ideal system.

Ekert91 (E91): Entanglement and the CHSH Test¶↑ Contents

Ekert91 uses entangled photon pairs and tests correlations that cannot be reproduced by local hidden-variable models under the standard assumptions of a Bell test. In this project it is a protocol-level teaching demonstration; its key-rate/security model is not substituted into the later DPS-FSO calculations. A convenient state is the singlet |Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2. For identical measurement axes the outcomes are ideally anti-correlated; Bob can invert his key bits under the stated mapping.

Alice and Bob independently select from three measurement observables each. Designated matching-direction pairs, such as (A₁,B₁) and (A₃,B₃) in the notebook convention, form sifted-key candidates. Other setting pairs estimate the CHSH correlators. They disclose the measurement settings, rather than publishing the undisclosed key outcomes. The appropriate security and parameter-estimation tests must succeed before reconciliation and privacy amplification.

S = | ⟨A₁B₃⟩ + ⟨A₁B₂⟩ + ⟨A₂B₃⟩ − ⟨A₂B₂⟩ |

Classical CHSH bound: S ≤ 2 | Ideal quantum setting: S = 2√2 ≈ 2.828

For the settings used in the notebook, the first three CHSH correlators equal −1/√2 and the fourth equals +1/√2, yielding |S| = 2√2. A measured value above 2 violates the specified classical CHSH bound, but a violation alone is not a complete deployment security proof. A measured S ≤ 2 fails this particular entanglement-security test; it is not automatic evidence of a specific attacker.

Coherent One-Way (COW): Time Bins and Coherence Monitoring¶↑ Contents

Coherent One-Way QKD transmits weak coherent pulses arranged in pairs of time slots. In the notebook convention, |0⟩ denotes a vacuum slot and |α⟩ a coherent optical pulse. Information is encoded by the occupied time slot: bit 0 uses (|0⟩,|α⟩), while bit 1 uses (|α⟩,|0⟩). Null (vacuum/vacuum) slots and control (pulse/pulse) slots can also be defined for teaching and monitoring purposes.

COW bit 0: (|0⟩, |α⟩) COW bit 1: (|α⟩, |0⟩)

Bob divides the incoming optical signal between a data detector and an interferometric monitoring line. The data detector determines which time bin is occupied; the delay interferometer tests coherence between adjacent non-empty pulses. Coherence monitoring is essential because simply detecting the pulse arrival time does not test every disturbance relevant to a coherent-state protocol. COW avoids the random basis switching used in BB84, but it has its own security assumptions and limitations.

Table 5. COW pulses.

Pair pattern Interpretation Receiver observation
Vacuum, pulse Bit 0 Detection in later slot on data line.
Pulse, vacuum Bit 1 Detection in earlier slot on data line.
Pulse, pulse Coherence/control sequence Relevant interference on monitoring line.
Vacuum, vacuum Null sequence No intentionally transmitted data pulse.

Comparison to DPS: both exploit coherent pulse sequences and avoid BB84-style basis switching. COW data bits are encoded primarily by time-slot occupancy, whereas DPS encodes the bit in the relative phase of successive pulses and reads it with a one-slot-delay interferometer. The notebook simulates COW as a teaching protocol; Section 12 and the satellite calculations remain DPS-based.

DPS-QKD Differential Phase Encoding¶↑ Contents

Differential Phase Shift Quantum Key Distribution (DPS QKD) uses a sequence of weak coherent optical pulses.

Unlike BB84, information is not encoded in the absolute polarization of each individual photon.

Instead, the secret bit is encoded in the phase difference between two consecutive optical pulses.

For pulse kk,

ak=μeiϕka_{k} = \sqrt{\mu}e^{i\phi_{k}}

where:

• μ\mu is the mean photon number per pulse

• ϕk\phi_{k} is the optical phase of pulse kk

The information bit is determined by the phase difference between consecutive pulses:

bk−1=ϕk−ϕk−1π(mod2)b_{k - 1} = \frac{\phi_{k} - \phi_{k - 1}}{\pi}(mod2)

The encoding rule is:

• Bit 0 -> no phase change

Δϕ=0\Delta\phi = 0

• Bit 1 -> phase change of π\pi

Δϕ=π\Delta\phi = \pi

Therefore, Alice does not encode a bit into one isolated pulse.

Instead:

One DPS bit is represented by the relative phase between two consecutive pulses\text{One~DPS~bit~is~represented~by~the~relative~phase~between~two~consecutive~pulses}

For example, consider the DPS data bits:

1 0 1 1 0

We start with an arbitrary reference phase:

Cascade: Interactive Parity-Based Reconciliation¶↑ Contents

Cascade divides Alice’s and Bob’s sifted strings into blocks, exchanges block parities over an authenticated public channel, and locates an odd-error block by repeated bisection. After one error is corrected, earlier partitions can be revisited because previously hidden even-error combinations may become detectable. Practical Cascade uses several passes, randomly permuted block assignments and carefully chosen block lengths.

Parity(block) = (Σ bit values in the block) mod 2

Worked concept: if Alice’s block parity is 0 and Bob’s is 1, an odd number of discrepancies is present. Divide the block in half, compare one half’s parity, select the half containing the discrepancy, and continue until a single bit is located. The public parity transcript leaks information and must be included in the privacy-amplification budget. The notebook’s short example demonstrates parity search logic, not a throughput-optimized production implementation.

Winnow: Parity Tests with Hamming Syndromes¶↑ Contents

Winnow combines a block-parity check with a Hamming-code syndrome. If a parity mismatch is detected and a single-error assumption is appropriate, a short syndrome difference can identify the differing bit with fewer interactive searches than Cascade. In the notebook’s illustrative Hamming (7,4) example, four information bits produce a seven-bit codeword and a three-bit parity-check syndrome.

Δs = Hx_Aᵀ ⊕ Hx_Bᵀ = Heᵀ (all operations over GF(2))

For an error vector e containing one nonzero position, Δs equals the corresponding column of H and identifies the candidate error location. A nonzero syndrome does not guarantee a correct one-bit correction if multiple errors are present; practical protocols include block screening, privacy leakage accounting and verification. The notebook’s small Winnow cell is a structural demonstration, not an end-to-end measured reconciliation efficiency result.

Polar-Code Reconciliation and Frozen-Bit Design¶↑ Contents

Polar codes use channel polarization to create synthesized bit-channels with differing reliabilities. The most reliable positions carry information bits, whereas less reliable positions are treated as frozen; decoder design uses reliability information from the observed channel/QBER. For block length N = 2ⁿ, the basic transform is constructed from repeated Kronecker products of the binary kernel F = [[1,0],[1,1]].

G_N = F⊗n over GF(2), with F = [[1,0],[1,1]]

The notebook illustrates an N = 8 polarization transform and a sample information/frozen-position assignment. It does not establish a production polar code reliability sequence, finite-length reconciliation leakage or a full successive-cancellation/list decoder. The correct practical comparison among Cascade, Winnow, LDPC and Polar depends on code rate, decoder implementation, channel conditions, interaction latency, information leakage and verification failure probability.

Error correction makes Alice's and Bob's reconciled keys identical, but it does not remove Eve's possible partial knowledge of the key.

Eavesdropping (Eve) and QBER in DPS-QKD¶↑ Contents

This is an example to demonstrate how loss of the relative-phase information used in Differential Phase Shift QKD (DPS-QKD) can increase Bob’s Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER). It is a simplified numerical disturbance model, not a proof that every eavesdropping strategy causes the same error rate.

DPS detector model

Alice encodes each bit using the relative phase between consecutive coherent pulses: 0 for bit 0 and π for bit 1. Bob’s one-bit-delay interferometer directs the interference to detector D0 or D1. With visibility V = 0.96 and no other noise, the expected wrong-detector probability for the correct phase is:

q₀ = (1 − V) / 2 = (1 − 0.96) / 2 = 0.02 = 2%

Suppose Eve replaces some received symbols with pulses whose relative phases are independent random choices of 0 or π. For the affected detected events, the resend phase matches Alice’s encoded phase half the time and reverses it half the time:

qₑ = ½[(1 − V)/2] + ½[(1 + V)/2] = ½ = 50%

Let f be the fraction of Bob’s accepted detection events affected by this random-phase replacement (rather than the fraction of individual transmitted pulses). For equal detection-event weighting, the expected combined QBER is:

QBER(f) = (1 − f)q₀ + f qₑ = 0.02 + 0.48f

Worked example: 10,000 accepted DPS detections

If 10% of accepted detections are affected (f = 0.10), 9,000 remain at the normal 2% error level and 1,000 have an expected 50% error level:

Expected errors = (9,000 × 0.02) + (1,000 × 0.50) = 180 + 500 = 680

QBER = 680 / 10,000 × 100 = 6.8%

Weak Coherent Source and Poisson Distribution¶↑ Contents

A practical DPS transmitter normally uses a heavily attenuated laser rather than an ideal source that emits exactly one photon on demand. Practical single-photon sources do exist; this choice describes the weak-coherent-pulse transmitter modeled here, not a claim that single-photon sources are merely theoretical.

A laser pulse is well described by a coherent state. If its mean photon number is μ\mu, then a photon-number measurement does not always return μ\mu photons. Instead, the number of photons varies randomly from pulse to pulse according to a Poisson distribution:

P(n)=e−μμnn!.P(n) = e^{- \mu}\frac{\mu^{n}}{n!}.

Here:

  • n=0,1,2,…n = 0,1,2,\ldots is the actual photon count in one pulse,

  • μ\mu is the average number of photons per pulse over many pulses,

  • P(n)P(n) is the probability that a pulse contains exactly nn photons.

So μ=0.5\mu = 0.5 does not mean every pulse contains half a photon. It means that after sending many pulses, the average photon count approaches 0.5 photons per pulse. Every individual pulse still contains an integer number of photons: 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on.

Yield and Photon-Number Gain

A yield answers the question:

If Alice emitted exactly nn photons, what is the probability that Bob registers a detection?

For a vacuum pulse, the only event in this simplified model is a dark/background detection:

Y0=Pd.Y_{0} = P_{d}.

For n≥1n \geq 1 photons:

Yn=1−(1−η)n.Y_{n} = 1 - (1 - \eta)^{n}.

This is easy to interpret. Each photon independently has probability η\eta of being detected. The probability that none of the nn photons is detected is (1−η)n(1 - \eta)^{n}, so the probability of at least one detection is its complement.

For example, if η=0.1\eta = 0.1:

Y1=1−0.9=0.1,Y_{1} = 1 - 0.9 = 0.1,

Y2=1−0.92=0.19,Y_{2} = 1 - {0.9}^{2} = 0.19,

Y3=1−0.93=0.271.Y_{3} = 1 - {0.9}^{3} = 0.271.

A pulse with more photons therefore has more chances to trigger a threshold detector.

The Poisson probability of emitting nn photons is:

pn=e−μμnn!.p_{n} = e^{- \mu}\frac{\mu^{n}}{n!}.

The gain of the nn-photon class is:

Gn=pnYn.G_{n} = p_{n}Y_{n}.

This combines two probabilities:

  1. Alice emits an nn-photon pulse;

  2. Bob detects it.

The main gain classes are:

G0=e−μPd,G_{0} = e^{- \mu}P_{d},

G1=μe−μη,G_{1} = \mu e^{- \mu}\eta,

and

G≥2=∑n=2∞pn[1−(1−η)n].G_{\geq 2} = \sum_{n = 2}^{\infty}p_{n}\left\lbrack 1 - (1 - \eta)^{n} \right\rbrack.

The multi-photon sum can be evaluated efficiently as:

G≥2=1−e−μη−G1.G_{\geq 2} = 1 - e^{- \mu\eta} - G_{1}.

For the current sample point:

G0≈3.44×10−6,G_{0} \approx 3.44 \times 10^{- 6},

G1≈4.47×10−4,G_{1} \approx 4.47 \times 10^{- 4},

Binary Entropy and Modeled SKR

The binary entropy function is:

h2(x)=−xlog2x−(1−x)log2(1−x).h_{2}(x) = - x\log_{2}x - (1 - x)\log_{2}(1 - x).

It converts an error probability into an information penalty. A perfectly predictable binary variable has entropy 0, while a completely random binary variable with probability 0.5 has entropy 1 bit.

The modeled per-gate key-rate proxy is:

r=12[G1(1−h2(Qph))−GfECh2(Qbit)].r = \frac{1}{2}\left\lbrack G_{1}\left( 1 - h_{2}(Q_{ph}) \right) - Gf_{EC}h_{2}(Q_{bit}) \right\rbrack.

The two main terms are:

  • Privacy term: G1(1−h2(Qph))G_{1}(1 - h_{2}(Q_{ph})),

  • Error-correction cost: GfECh2(Qbit)Gf_{EC}h_{2}(Q_{bit}).

The factor fEC>1f_{EC} > 1 represents practical error correction being less efficient than the Shannon limit.

If the expression becomes negative, this study clips it to zero because a negative usable key rate has no physical meaning.

Gauss-Hermite Quadrature for Log-Normal Fading

Atmospheric turbulence causes the received optical power to fluctuate with time.

Instead of assuming one fixed channel efficiency,

η=ηstatic,\eta = \eta_{static},

we model the instantaneous efficiency as:

η=ηstaticF\eta = \eta_{static}F

where:

  • ηstatic\eta_{static} is the FSO efficiency without turbulence

  • FF is a random multiplicative fading factor

For weak-to-moderate turbulence, FF is commonly represented by a log-normal random variable.

We define:

F=exp(−σ22+σZ)F = exp\left( \frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} + \sigma Z \right)

where:

ZN(0,1)ZN(0,1)

and σ\sigma controls the strength of the fluctuations.

Why is −σ22\frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} included?

The factor

−σ22\frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2}

normalizes the fading distribution so that:

E[F]=1.E\lbrack F\rbrack = 1.

This is useful because the turbulence model then represents fluctuations around the existing static link budget rather than adding another average attenuation term.

For example, if:

ηstatic=0.003\eta_{static} = 0.003

and the instantaneous fade is:

F=0.5,F = 0.5,

then:

η=0.003×0.5=0.0015.\eta = 0.003 \times 0.5 = 0.0015.

The received signal is temporarily weaker.

If instead:

F=1.5,F = 1.5,

then:

η=0.003×1.5=0.0045.\eta = 0.003 \times 1.5 = 0.0045.

The instantaneous received signal is stronger than its long-term average.

Example with σ=0.45\sigma = 0.45

For this study we use:

σ=0.45.\sigma = 0.45.

Notice that the central fading value is slightly below 1.

This follows from the asymmetry of the log-normal distribution.

A log-normal distribution is asymmetric. Many samples are below 1, while a smaller number of large positive fades pull the overall average back to:

E[F]=1.E\lbrack F\rbrack = 1.

Why do we need an average?

The DPS detector performance depends nonlinearly on channel efficiency.

For example:

G=G(η)G = G(\eta)

and:

Q=Q(η).Q = Q(\eta).

Since turbulence continuously changes η\eta, we need quantities such as:

E[G]E\lbrack G\rbrack

rather than evaluating the detector only once at the average channel efficiency.

In principle:

E[g(F)]=∫0∞g(F)p(F)dF.E\left\lbrack g(F) \right\rbrack = \int_{0}^{\infty}{g(F)p(F)dF.}

Monte Carlo simulation estimates this integral by generating thousands of random fading samples.

Gauss-Hermite quadrature provides another approach: it replaces the continuous Gaussian distribution with a small number of carefully selected deterministic points.

Applying the Five Fading States to the DPS Link

The purpose of Gauss-Hermite quadrature is not merely to reproduce the average fading factor.

For every fading state, the complete DPS channel model is recalculated.

At quadrature node ii:

ηi=ηstaticFi.\eta_{i} = \eta_{static}F_{i}.

The DPS detector then calculates:

Gi=G(ηi)G_{i} = G(\eta_{i})

and:

Qi=Q(ηi).Q_{i} = Q(\eta_{i}).

This means that a weak fading state produces a lower received signal, while a strong fading state produces a larger detection probability.

Qpooled=∑iwiGiQi∑iwiGi.Q_{pooled} = \frac{\sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}Q_{i}}{\sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}}.

This is equivalent to:

Qpooled=expected erroneous detectionsexpected total detections.Q_{pooled} = \frac{\text{expected~erroneous~detections}}{\text{expected~total~detections}}.

In earlier sections we used only five Gauss-Hermite nodes so that every fading state and weight could be inspected manually. We now use 48 nodes for the numerical turbulence calculation.

Scientific Interpretation and Reproducibility Limits¶↑ Contents

The main contribution of this study is the use of machine learning to identify improved DPS-QKD operating parameters within the same physics evaluation budget. In the recorded simulation, the ML-assisted approach evaluated a much larger number of candidate configurations using an RBF surrogate model and identified a design with a higher modeled secret key rate, which was subsequently verified using the physics model. However, the conventional physics-based approach was faster in terms of overall execution time because the analytical calculations were relatively simple. ML-assisted optimization may offer greater computational benefits in more complex simulations involving Monte Carlo methods or detailed security analysis, although this remains to be investigated.

The optimization considers six parameters, including source mean photon number, receiver aperture, atmospheric turbulence, interference visibility, background detection probability and pointing loss. Some of these parameters can be adjusted during system design, while others depend on environmental conditions or hardware characteristics. Therefore, the best-performing configuration obtained from the simulation represents a theoretical optimum within the selected parameter ranges rather than a configuration that can necessarily be achieved in a practical installation. Further evaluation using multiple simulation runs and fixed environmental conditions would help establish the consistency and practical applicability of the results.

The simulation framework has also been implemented in GNU Octave, using the same six input parameters and an RBF surrogate model that predicts detector rate and QBER. Both implementations calculate the modeled secret key rate from these outputs and use equivalent physics evaluation budgets, with 300 evaluations for direct optimization and 150 training, 100 validation and 50 verification evaluations for the ML-assisted approach. The ML model additionally evaluates one million candidate configurations through batched predictions. Although the Python and GNU Octave implementations follow the same mathematical assumptions, differences in random sampling and numerical execution may result in variations in the optimized values and computational time.

How the Trusted-Key Relay Works¶↑ Contents

The satellite generates a new session key KK and publishes:

CA=K⊕KASC_{A} = K \oplus K_{AS}

and

CB=K⊕KSB.C_{B} = K \oplus K_{SB}.

The published relay ciphertexts must be carried over an authenticated classical channel so that an adversary cannot substitute or modify the relay messages without detection.

A recovers:

K=CA⊕KAS,K = C_{A} \oplus K_{AS},

while B recovers:

K=CB⊕KSB.K = C_{B} \oplus K_{SB}.

The satellite knows KK, so this architecture requires trust in the relay and secure key storage on the satellite.

If A-S produces BAB_{A} secure bits and S-B produces BBB_{B} secure bits, the maximum equal-length relayed key is bounded by:

Brelay≤min(BA,BB).B_{relay} \leq min(B_{A},B_{B}).

The weaker hop is the bottleneck because one one-time-pad bit is consumed on each hop for every relayed session-key bit.

Cryptographic protection boundary and integration model¶↑ Contents

The bank's storage and application replication flows remain conventional data traffic. In the proposed architecture, supported encryption gateways or compatible application/transport security endpoints apply authenticated symmetric encryption, for example a correctly configured AES-256-based authenticated mode. A key-management layer identifies authorized endpoint pairs, handles key identifiers, rotation, access control, expiry, monitoring and audit, and transfers key material to the compatible encryption endpoints. I do not assume that IBM i, Oracle, IBM storage controllers or existing VPN appliances natively ingest QKD keys; those interfaces and vendors must be examined before a proof of concept.

Where compatible QKD equipment exists, separate optical quantum links could supply key material to authorized key-management entities. The original report identifies ETSI GS QKD 014 as a possible application-interface reference; actual device conformance and integration would need to be demonstrated. Existing fiber or IP paths would continue carrying encrypted replication traffic. A carrier data service does not automatically expose a transparent optical path suitable for QKD, and a public-internet VPN is not a quantum optical channel.

QKD-derived key material and a supported post-quantum key-establishment result could be incorporated under an explicitly validated hybrid key-derivation and authentication design. Merely concatenating keys or claiming a fallback is quantum-safe without a security analysis would be insufficient. Key generation, key usage and rekey intervals must be sized using measured application traffic, equipment limits and outage requirements. The present simulation does not demonstrate full-rate one-time-pad encryption, production replication throughput or an approved hybrid cryptographic protocol.

Conclusion and Future Work¶↑ Contents

This project presents a comprehensive simulation-based study of Differential Phase Shift Quantum Key Distribution (DPS-QKD) over Free-Space Optical (FSO) channels, progressing from fundamental QKD principles to terrestrial optical links, atmospheric turbulence, machine-learning-assisted optimization, satellite communication, and potential banking applications.

The study first examines five important QKD protocols—BB84, BBM92, Ekert91 (E91), Coherent One-Way (COW), and DPS—to establish their operating principles, encoding methods, and security considerations. It also explores quantum error estimation, information reconciliation using Cascade, Winnow, LDPC, and Polar codes, and privacy amplification through Toeplitz hashing. These concepts provide the theoretical foundation for the detailed DPS-QKD simulation.

The developed DPS-FSO model incorporates weak coherent optical pulses, differential phase encoding, one-slot-delay interferometry, photon detection, optical attenuation, pointing losses, detector imperfections, and log-normal atmospheric turbulence. The numerical framework evaluates raw detection rates, Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER), and a modeled Secret Key Rate (SKR) using a generic asymptotic entropy-based post-processing approximation. Detection-weighted statistical averaging is applied to obtain more representative results under fluctuating channel conditions.

A major component of the project is the application of Machine Learning (ML) to constrained DPS-FSO parameter optimization. A six-parameter Radial Basis Function (RBF) surrogate model is trained using physics-generated data to predict detector rates and QBER, from which modeled SKR is calculated. To evaluate the contribution of ML fairly, the study compares direct physics optimization and ML-assisted optimization using an equal budget of 300 physics-model evaluations.

The ML-assisted approach evaluates one million candidate parameter combinations and physically verifies its most promising designs. In the recorded single-seed experiment, ML-assisted optimization identified a design achieving approximately 1,918 modeled SKR bit/s, compared with 1,419 modeled SKR bit/s from the direct physics search, representing an improvement of approximately 35.2% in the selected modeled key rate.

The study further extends DPS-QKD modeling to Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite downlinks, trusted satellite relays, and multi-satellite networks. These simulations demonstrate the effects of orbital geometry, limited contact windows, optical losses, and link availability on accumulated key generation. A banking application case study illustrates how such technologies could potentially support long-term cryptographic key protection between geographically separated data centers.

Overall, the project establishes an integrated framework for studying DPS-QKD performance under realistic optical-channel constraints and demonstrates how physics-based simulation and machine learning can be combined to explore engineering design alternatives. It provides a foundation for further research into secure terrestrial and satellite quantum communication systems.

Future Work¶↑ Contents

Future research shall focus on improving the accuracy, security, and practical implementation of DPS-QKD over FSO channels.

DPS-specific security: Implement a composable finite-key security framework that considers multiphoton effects, eavesdropping risks, reconciliation leakage, authentication, and privacy amplification to obtain more rigorous SKR estimates.

Information reconciliation: Extend Cascade, Winnow, LDPC, and Polar codes into practical implementations and compare their efficiency, error-correction performance, and information leakage.

Atmospheric modeling: Incorporate Monte Carlo simulations, beam wander, scintillation, pointing errors, and real atmospheric measurements, particularly from Bahrain and nearby regions.

Machine learning: Investigate active learning, Bayesian optimization, multiple random seeds, and computationally intensive physics models to evaluate ML efficiency and robustness under realistic environmental conditions.

Satellite networks: Introduce realistic orbital trajectories, satellite tracking, contact scheduling, trusted-node key storage, and dynamic routing to improve end-to-end key-distribution estimates.

Experimental and banking applications: Validate the simulations using physical optical and quantum components and explore integration with banking encryption systems, hardware security modules, and key-management infrastructure.
