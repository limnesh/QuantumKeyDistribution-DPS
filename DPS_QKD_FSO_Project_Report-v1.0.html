Abstract¶

This project develops a reproducible numerical framework for differential phase shift quantum key distribution (DPS-QKD) over free-space optical (FSO) channels. The report begins with the long-term confidentiality problem and establishes the roles of quantum communication, authenticated classical discussion and conventional encrypted data transport. BB84, BBM92 and DPS teaching examples introduce the main protocol approaches; QBER estimation, syndrome-based information reconciliation and Toeplitz privacy amplification then establish the common key post-processing chain. The study then returns to DPS for the interferometer, detector-click and photon-statistics models before evaluating a 20 km terrestrial optical link, atmospheric fading, machine-learning-assisted design search, LEO satellite, trusted-relay, multi-satellite and banking application scenarios.

At the 20 km baseline, the total efficiency is 0.003464884, physical raw detections are approximately 5,236/s, and physical click-conditioned QBER is 1.374%. The separate compact model gives 3.180% modeled bit QBER and 843.5 modeled bit/s at a 10 MHz clock. The turbulence-pooled result is 843.7 modeled bit/s. An RBF kernel-ridge surrogate reaches held-out R² values of 0.9921 for rate and 0.9755 for modeled QBER; its proposed operating point yields 2,940.0 modeled bit/s under the original physics recheck. The sampled single-LEO pass provides about 95,195 modeled proxy bits. Trusted relay and network results are interpreted as minimum integrated hop budgets.

Introduction and Problem Statement¶

This section defines the long-term confidentiality problem that motivates the project. It first explains the quantum threat to widely used public-key mechanisms, then positions post-quantum cryptography and QKD as complementary quantum-safe approaches before stating the project objectives and scope.

Long-Term Confidentiality and the Quantum Threat¶

Modern digital communication relies heavily on cryptographic algorithms to protect sensitive information transmitted across public and private networks. Although current encryption systems provide strong protection against conventional computing attacks, many widely deployed security protocols depend on public-key algorithms such as RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) for key establishment and authentication. The rapid advancement of quantum computing introduces a significant long-term security concern because a sufficiently powerful, fault-tolerant quantum computer could break these algorithms using Shor’s algorithm. This creates the Harvest-Now, Decrypt-Later (HNDL) threat, in which adversaries intercept and store encrypted communications today with the intention of decrypting them when future quantum computing capabilities become available. Information requiring confidentiality for many years, including financial records, healthcare data, government communications, intellectual property and critical infrastructure information, is particularly exposed to this risk. Although strong symmetric encryption such as AES-256 is not immediately considered broken by the same quantum attack, the compromise of vulnerable key-establishment mechanisms could expose the keys used to protect recorded communications. Consequently, relying exclusively on conventional, quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography may not provide sufficient long-term confidentiality.

Shor’s Algorithm¶

RSA relies on a problem that is easy to perform in one direction and computationally difficult to reverse: multiplying two very large prime numbers is straightforward, while recovering those factors from their product is impractical for current conventional computers at cryptographic sizes. Shor’s algorithm changes this security assumption because a sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer could factor large integers efficiently. NIST notes that factoring sufficiently large integers has been estimated to require extremely long times on conventional computers, whereas a cryptographically relevant quantum computer could reduce the task dramatically; exact RSA-breaking times depend on the assumed quantum architecture, error correction and available resources. Today’s quantum computers cannot break RSA-2048

Quantum-Safe Approaches: PQC and QKD¶

This problem motivates the investigation of quantum-resistant approaches, including post-quantum cryptography and Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), which uses quantum-mechanical principles to establish shared secret keys. When appropriately implemented alongside authenticated communication, secure key management and strong symmetric encryption, QKD offers an alternative key-distribution mechanism for protecting sensitive information against future cryptographic threats.

Example: Suppose an organization has data that must remain confidential for 10 years. An attacker records encrypted traffic today. If the session keys were established using a public-key method that is vulnerable to a future quantum computer, and a sufficiently capable quantum-computing service becomes available before the 10-year confidentiality period expires, the attacker could potentially recover the old session secrets and decrypt the stored traffic while the information is still sensitive.

Possible organizational impact includes:

  • exposure of confidential customer, financial, technical, or business data;

  • breach of contractual or regulatory confidentiality obligations;

  • loss of intellectual property or competitive information;

  • incident-response, legal, notification, and recovery costs;

  • reputational damage even though the original traffic was captured years earlier.

The purpose of quantum-safe key establishment is therefore not only to protect communications against attacks available today, but also to protect information whose required secrecy lifetime extends into the period when large fault-tolerant quantum computers may exist.

Fundamentals of Quantum Key Distribution¶

This section introduces the communication model common to the QKD protocols used in this study. It distinguishes the quantum channel, the authenticated classical channel and the classical post-processing stages that transform raw detection events into shared key material.

Quantum and Classical Channels, Threats and Authentication¶

Depending on the protocol, Alice prepares quantum signals for Bob (for example BB84/DPS) or Alice and Bob measure signals distributed from an entangled source (for example BBM92). They use an authenticated classical channel for the required public discussion—such as basis information, detection positions, parameter estimation, reconciliation data and verification—without publishing the undisclosed raw key. They estimate errors, reconcile, verify equality, and compress the key to limit Eve's information. Eve may observe or manipulate the optical channel and public discussion; authentication blocks an otherwise fatal man-in-the-middle impersonation. Photon loss or errors alone cannot identify Eve: hardware imperfections also cause them. Real devices require security analysis against side channels.

Example of why authentication matters: Without it, Eve can separately negotiate a key with Alice and another with Bob, then read and forward messages. A QBER check in each forged session need not expose her, because Eve is acting as an endpoint of two separate sessions rather than merely measuring and resending individual signals.

Why QKD Still Requires Authentication¶

QKD can reveal disturbances under its protocol and device assumptions, but it does not automatically prove the identity of the communicating parties.

The classical channel used for:

  • Basis announcements,

  • Parameter estimation,

  • Syndrome exchange,

  • Error correction, and

  • Privacy-amplification coordination

must therefore be authenticated.

If the classical channel is not authenticated, Eve can perform a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack.

Instead of allowing Alice and Bob to establish one common key,

Alice↔BobAlice \longleftrightarrow Bob

Eve can create two independent connections:

Alice↔EveAlice \longleftrightarrow Eve

and

Eve↔BobEve \longleftrightarrow Bob

Alice may believe that her key is shared with Bob, while it is actually shared with Eve.
Bob may similarly believe that his key is shared with Alice, while it is actually shared with Eve.

Therefore, matching keys within a QKD session do not by themselves prove the identity of the remote party.

QKD Key-Processing Flow¶

At a high level, QKD proceeds from quantum-state preparation or entangled-state measurement to valid-event selection, parameter estimation and QBER sampling, information reconciliation, key verification and privacy amplification. These steps are coordinated over an authenticated classical channel without publishing the undisclosed raw key; the resulting shared key can then be supplied to conventional cryptographic protection.

BB84 Protocol Demonstration¶

BB84 encodes each classical bit using one of two randomly selected quantum bases.

Two BB84 bases

The rectilinear basis (Z basis) uses:

  • Bit 0 -> Horizontal polarization, H

  • Bit 1 -> Vertical polarization, V

The diagonal basis (X basis) uses:

  • Bit 0 -> +45 degree polarization, D

  • Bit 1 -> -45 degree polarization, A

In this simulation we represent the bases numerically as:

  • 0 -> Z basis (H/V)

  • 1 -> X basis (D/A)

Alice randomly generates:

  1. A raw binary key.

  2. A random basis for every bit.

  3. The corresponding quantum state.

Bob does not know Alice's basis while measuring the incoming photons. He therefore independently chooses a random measurement basis for every photon.

If Bob chooses the same basis as Alice, he obtains Alice's bit correctly in this idealized simulation.

If Bob chooses the wrong basis, quantum mechanics gives a random result, so Bob has a 50% probability of obtaining either 0 or 1.

After the quantum transmission is complete, Alice and Bob publicly announce only their basis choices over an authenticated classical channel.

They do not reveal their secret bit values.

They keep only positions where their bases matched. This process is called basis sifting.

For random independent basis choices,

P(basis match)=12P(\text{basis~match}) = \frac{1}{2}

BBM92 Protocol Demonstration¶

BBM92 is an entanglement-based quantum key distribution protocol.

Unlike BB84, Alice does not prepare a classical bit and encode it into a photon.

Instead, an entangled-photon source generates pairs of photons. One photon is sent to Alice and the other photon is sent to Bob.

In this educational example, the source produces the Bell state:

Φ+⟩=HH⟩+VV⟩2\Phi^{+ \rangle = \frac{HH\rangle + VV\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}}

The same state can also be written in the diagonal basis as:

Φ+⟩=DD⟩+AA⟩2\Phi^{+ \rangle = \frac{DD\rangle + AA\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}}

DPS-QKD Differential Phase Encoding¶

Differential Phase Shift Quantum Key Distribution (DPS QKD) uses a sequence of weak coherent optical pulses.

Unlike BB84, information is not encoded in the absolute polarization of each individual photon.

Instead, the secret bit is encoded in the phase difference between two consecutive optical pulses.

For pulse kk,

ak=μeiϕka_{k} = \sqrt{\mu}e^{i\phi_{k}}

where:

• μ\mu is the mean photon number per pulse

• ϕk\phi_{k} is the optical phase of pulse kk

The information bit is determined by the phase difference between consecutive pulses:

bk−1=ϕk−ϕk−1π(mod2)b_{k - 1} = \frac{\phi_{k} - \phi_{k - 1}}{\pi}(mod2)

The encoding rule is:

• Bit 0 -> no phase change

Δϕ=0\Delta\phi = 0

• Bit 1 -> phase change of π\pi

Δϕ=π\Delta\phi = \pi

Therefore, Alice does not encode a bit into one isolated pulse.

Instead:

One DPS bit is represented by the relative phase between two consecutive pulses\text{One~DPS~bit~is~represented~by~the~relative~phase~between~two~consecutive~pulses}

For example, consider the DPS data bits:

1 0 1 1 0

We start with an arbitrary reference phase:

ϕ0=0\phi_{0} = 0

and generate every following pulse phase according to:

ϕk=ϕk−1+πbk−1(mod2π)\phi_{k} = \phi_{k - 1} + \pi b_{k - 1}(mod2\pi)

This produces six optical pulses for five DPS bits.

Bob uses a delay-line Mach-Zehnder interferometer with a delay equal to one pulse period.

This causes pulse kk to interfere with pulse k−1k - 1.

In an ideal system:

• Equal phases -> Detector 0 clicks

• Opposite phases -> Detector 1 clicks

Therefore:

D0→0D_{0} \rightarrow 0

and

D1→1D_{1} \rightarrow 1

This first example assumes an ideal optical channel with no loss, detector noise, turbulence or eavesdropper.

The five-bit sequence 10110 specifies the relative phase differences of a six-pulse train. The initial pulse is a phase reference.

Privacy Amplification with Toeplitz Hashing¶

Error correction makes Alice's and Bob's reconciled keys identical, but it does not remove Eve's possible partial knowledge of the key.

Privacy amplification compresses the reconciled key using a random, publicly known universal hash function. A commonly used construction in QKD is a binary Toeplitz matrix, whose values are constant along each diagonal.

For a reconciled key:

x∈{0,1}nx \in \{ 0,1\}^{n}

Alice and Bob calculate the final key as:

K=Tx(mod2)K = Tx(mod2)

where TT is an l×nl \times n Toeplitz matrix.

For this example:

n=16,l=8n = 16,l = 8

Therefore:

T∈GF(2)8×16T \in GF(2)^{8 \times 16}

An 8×168 \times 16 Toeplitz matrix requires:

n+l−1=16+8−1=23n + l - 1 = 16 + 8 - 1 = 23

The Toeplitz matrix and its seed may be public. Security does not depend on keeping TT secret. In the implemented calculation the seed is fixed only to make the demonstration reproducible; a real protocol chooses the universal hash according to its security procedure.

Weak Coherent Source and Poisson Distribution¶

A practical DPS transmitter normally uses a heavily attenuated laser rather than an ideal source that emits exactly one photon on demand. Practical single-photon sources do exist; this choice describes the weak-coherent-pulse transmitter modeled here, not a claim that single-photon sources are merely theoretical.

A laser pulse is well described by a coherent state. If its mean photon number is μ\mu, then a photon-number measurement does not always return μ\mu photons. Instead, the number of photons varies randomly from pulse to pulse according to a Poisson distribution:

P(n)=e−μμnn!.P(n) = e^{- \mu}\frac{\mu^{n}}{n!}.

Here:

  • n=0,1,2,…n = 0,1,2,\ldots is the actual photon count in one pulse,

  • μ\mu is the average number of photons per pulse over many pulses,

  • P(n)P(n) is the probability that a pulse contains exactly nn photons.

So μ=0.5\mu = 0.5 does not mean every pulse contains half a photon. It means that after sending many pulses, the average photon count approaches 0.5 photons per pulse. Every individual pulse still contains an integer number of photons: 0, 1, 2, 3, and so on.

Yield and Photon-Number Gain

A yield answers the question:

If Alice emitted exactly nn photons, what is the probability that Bob registers a detection?

For a vacuum pulse, the only event in this simplified model is a dark/background detection:

Y0=Pd.Y_{0} = P_{d}.

For n≥1n \geq 1 photons:

Yn=1−(1−η)n.Y_{n} = 1 - (1 - \eta)^{n}.

This is easy to interpret. Each photon independently has probability η\eta of being detected. The probability that none of the nn photons is detected is (1−η)n(1 - \eta)^{n}, so the probability of at least one detection is its complement.

For example, if η=0.1\eta = 0.1:

Y1=1−0.9=0.1,Y_{1} = 1 - 0.9 = 0.1,

A pulse with more photons therefore has more chances to trigger a threshold detector.

The binary entropy function is:

Gauss-Hermite Quadrature for Log-Normal Fading

Atmospheric turbulence causes the received optical power to fluctuate with time.

Instead of assuming one fixed channel efficiency,

η=ηstatic,\eta = \eta_{static},

we model the instantaneous efficiency as:

η=ηstaticF\eta = \eta_{static}F

where:

  • ηstatic\eta_{static} is the FSO efficiency without turbulence

  • FF is a random multiplicative fading factor

For weak-to-moderate turbulence, FF is commonly represented by a log-normal random variable.

We define:

F=exp(−σ22+σZ)F = exp\left( \frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} + \sigma Z \right)

where:

ZN(0,1)ZN(0,1)

and σ\sigma controls the strength of the fluctuations.

Why is −σ22\frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} included?

The factor

−σ22\frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2}

normalizes the fading distribution so that:

E[F]=1.E\lbrack F\rbrack = 1.

This is useful because the turbulence model then represents fluctuations around the existing static link budget rather than adding another average attenuation term.

For example, if:

ηstatic=0.003\eta_{static} = 0.003

and the instantaneous fade is:

F=0.5,F = 0.5,

then:

η=0.003×0.5=0.0015.\eta = 0.003 \times 0.5 = 0.0015.

The received signal is temporarily weaker.

If instead:

F=1.5,F = 1.5,

then:

η=0.003×1.5=0.0045.\eta = 0.003 \times 1.5 = 0.0045.

The instantaneous received signal is stronger than its long-term average.

Example with σ=0.45\sigma = 0.45

For this study we use:

σ=0.45.\sigma = 0.45.

If Z=0Z = 0, which represents the center of the underlying Gaussian distribution:

F=exp(−0.4522)F = exp\left( - \frac{{0.45}^{2}}{2} \right)

Notice that the central fading value is slightly below 1.

This follows from the asymmetry of the log-normal distribution.

A log-normal distribution is asymmetric. Many samples are below 1, while a smaller number of large positive fades pull the overall average back to:

E[F]=1.E\lbrack F\rbrack = 1.

Why do we need an average?

The DPS detector performance depends nonlinearly on channel efficiency.

For example:

G=G(η)G = G(\eta)

and:

Q=Q(η).Q = Q(\eta).

Since turbulence continuously changes η\eta, we need quantities such as:

E[G]E\lbrack G\rbrack

rather than evaluating the detector only once at the average channel efficiency.

In principle:

E[g(F)]=∫0∞g(F)p(F)dF.E\left\lbrack g(F) \right\rbrack = \int_{0}^{\infty}{g(F)p(F)dF.}

Monte Carlo simulation estimates this integral by generating thousands of random fading samples.

Gauss-Hermite quadrature provides another approach: it replaces the continuous Gaussian distribution with a small number of carefully selected deterministic points.

F=exp(−σ22+σZ)F = exp\left( \frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} + \sigma Z \right)

E[g(F)]=12π∫−∞∞g(e−σ22+σz)e−z22dz.E\left\lbrack g(F) \right\rbrack = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{- \infty}^{\infty}g\left( e^{\frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} + \sigma z} \right)e^{\frac{- z^{2}}{2}}dz.

e−x2.e^{- x^{2}}.

Applying the Five Fading States to the DPS Link

The purpose of Gauss-Hermite quadrature is not merely to reproduce the average fading factor.

For every fading state, the complete DPS channel model is recalculated.

At quadrature node ii:

ηi=ηstaticFi.\eta_{i} = \eta_{static}F_{i}.

The DPS detector then calculates:

Gi=G(ηi)G_{i} = G(\eta_{i})

and:

Qi=Q(ηi).Q_{i} = Q(\eta_{i}).

This means that a weak fading state produces a lower received signal, while a strong fading state produces a larger detection probability.

Because the number of detections is different for each fading state, the overall QBER should not normally be calculated as a simple arithmetic average of the individual QBER values.

Instead, errors are pooled according to the number of detections:

Qpooled=∑iwiGiQi∑iwiGi.Q_{pooled} = \frac{\sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}Q_{i}}{\sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}}.

This is equivalent to:

Accurate Turbulence Integration with 48 Gauss-Hermite Nodes

In earlier sections we used only five Gauss-Hermite nodes so that every fading state and weight could be inspected manually. We now use 48 nodes for the numerical turbulence calculation.

The value 48 has no special physical meaning. It is simply a sufficiently high quadrature order for this smooth weak-to-moderate log-normal example while remaining computationally inexpensive.

For each quadrature node ii:

Fi=exp(−σ22+2σxi)F_{i} = exp\left( \frac{- \sigma^{2}}{2} + \sqrt{2}\sigma x_{i} \right)

and the instantaneous channel efficiency is:

ηi=min(ηstaticFi,1).\eta_{i} = min(\eta_{static}F_{i},1).

The cap at 1 enforces the physical requirement that total efficiency cannot exceed 100%.

The production calculation therefore follows this chain:

Gauss-Hermite node -> fading factor -> instantaneous eta -> DPS detector -> gain/QBER/rate -> weighted average

The important point is that we do not replace turbulence by one average fading number. Detector gain, QBER, binary entropy, and the modeled rate are nonlinear functions of transmission. In general:

E[g(η)]≠g(E[η]).E\left\lbrack g(\eta) \right\rbrack \neq g(E\lbrack\eta\rbrack).

So the nonlinear DPS model is evaluated at every quadrature state before the results are combined.

Detection-Weighted QBER

A simple average of the individual QBER values is not the correct way to combine a fading channel because different fading states produce different numbers of detections.

For node ii:

Cdet,i=wiGiC_{det,i} = w_{i}G_{i}

is its expected contribution to total detections, while:

Cerr,i=wiGiQiC_{err,i} = w_{i}G_{i}Q_{i}

is its expected contribution to erroneous detections.

Therefore:

Ǵ=∑iwiGi\acute{G} = \sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}

and the pooled QBER is:

Qpooled=∑iwiGiQi∑iwiGi.Q_{pooled} = \frac{\sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}Q_{i}}{\sum_{i}^{}w_{i}G_{i}}.

Small example: if one state produces 1,000 detections at 2% QBER and another produces only 100 detections at 10% QBER, the arithmetic average is 6%, but the actual pooled result is:

1000(0.02)+100(0.10)1100=2.73%.\frac{1000(0.02) + 100(0.10)}{1100} = 2.73\text{\%}.

The study therefore reports both the simple weighted average for comparison and the physically more meaningful detection-weighted pooled QBER.

Two Ground Stations and One Trusted Satellite Relay¶

A single satellite cannot be treated as a passive optical mirror in this model. Instead, it establishes two independent QKD links at different times:

A↔SA \leftrightarrow S

and

S↔B.S \leftrightarrow B.

After full security processing, suppose the satellite shares:

• secure hop key KASK_{AS} with ground station A,

• secure hop key KSBK_{SB} with ground station B.

The contact windows do not need to overlap because the trusted satellite can store the two keys.

How the Trusted-Key Relay Works¶

The satellite generates a new session key KK and publishes:

CA=K⊕KASC_{A} = K \oplus K_{AS}

and

CB=K⊕KSB.C_{B} = K \oplus K_{SB}.

The published relay ciphertexts must be carried over an authenticated classical channel so that an adversary cannot substitute or modify the relay messages without detection.

A recovers:

K=CA⊕KAS,K = C_{A} \oplus K_{AS},

while B recovers:

K=CB⊕KSB.K = C_{B} \oplus K_{SB}.

The satellite knows KK, so this architecture requires trust in the relay and secure key storage on the satellite.

If A-S produces BAB_{A} secure bits and S-B produces BBB_{B} secure bits, the maximum equal-length relayed key is bounded by:

Brelay≤min(BA,BB).B_{relay} \leq min(B_{A},B_{B}).

The weaker hop is the bottleneck because one one-time-pad bit is consumed on each hop for every relayed session-key bit.

Three-Satellite Trusted Network and Route Budgets¶

The final topology extends the trusted-relay idea to several satellites.

Each optical edge accumulates its own key inventory:

A↔S0,S0↔S1,S1↔S2,S2↔B.A \leftrightarrow S_{0},S_{0} \leftrightarrow S_{1},S_{1} \leftrightarrow S_{2},S_{2} \leftrightarrow B.

Every satellite is treated as a trusted key-storage and forwarding node. This section does not model quantum repeaters, entanglement swapping or untrusted end-to-end QKD.

For an mm-edge trusted path, every delivered one-time-pad bit consumes one secure bit on each edge. Therefore:

Bpath≤min(B1,B2,…,Bm).B_{path} \leq min(B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{m}).

The path is constrained by its weakest accumulated hop inventory.

Banking Application Case Study: Bahrain Sample Bank¶

The following application case study places the four DPS-FSO simulation scenarios in the context of Bahrain Sample Bank (BSB), a fictional institution. The site locations, workloads, connectivity and continuity requirements are planning assumptions from the original report, not an audited customer network or a deployed quantum-security claim. No telecom-route survey, bank equipment inventory or measured site-specific optical model is available.

Case study environment and existing connectivity¶

BSB operates a primary data center at its headquarters in Seef, Bahrain, a disaster recovery (DR) site in Amwaj, Bahrain, and an offshore computing site in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Core banking workloads run on IBM i (AS/400), while the mobile banking environment uses Oracle. The case assumes that these workloads and their data reside on IBM storage infrastructure, with data synchronized from headquarters to the other computing sites. The precise IBM storage models, replication software, database replication topology, bandwidth and encryption termination points are not specified and therefore are not assumed in my analysis.

The existing HQ-to-DR data connection is a dedicated telecom-provider fiber service. Traffic from Bahrain to Riyadh is carried over site-to-site VPNs on the public internet. Numerous branches in Bahrain and Saudi Arabia also use site-to-site VPN connectivity. The approximately 14-15 km Seef-Amwaj and 420 km Bahrain-Riyadh figures are straight-line separations, not surveyed fiber route lengths or free-space satellite optical path lengths.

Operational context and quantum-related security problem¶

BSB performs two planned business continuity planning (BCP) drills each year. During each drill, production workloads operate from Amwaj DR for approximately two weeks before a controlled return to Seef HQ. Encryption and key management must therefore function when replication direction and production endpoints change. The design must protect HQ-to-DR synchronization, DR-to-HQ reverse synchronization, Bahrain-to-Riyadh transfers and relevant branch traffic without making banking availability dependent on clear weather or a satellite pass.

The application motivation is long-term confidentiality of archived traffic. QKD is considered as an additional potential source of key material; it does not encrypt the replication payload. Authenticated symmetric encryption and a validated key-establishment and authentication design remain necessary. Post-quantum or hybrid key establishment on conventional VPN infrastructure is a separate candidate workstream; the simulation does not implement it or assess current vendor support.

Application objective and specific research questions¶

My application objective is to evaluate a phased, quantum-safe key-establishment and data-in-transit encryption architecture for BSB that protects the replication of IBM i core banking and Oracle mobile banking information among Seef HQ, Amwaj DR and Riyadh, while preserving two-week DR operation and allowing selective expansion to branch connections.

  • Map the assumed bank links to the four current terrestrial and trusted satellite scenarios while distinguishing generic geometry from real bank locations.

  • Evaluate optical efficiency, physical and modeled QBER, rate proxies, sampled outage definitions and integrated link budgets under explicit assumptions.

  • Distinguish QKD key delivery from AES-256 authenticated encryption of the actual replication stream and identify practical KMS, HSM, encryption-gateway and authentication interfaces.

  • Assess trusted-relay and satellite-operator exposure explicitly; the current international-link models use DPS trusted nodes rather than an entangled-pair BBM92 link.

  • Define key continuity, reverse-replication and fallback requirements for the bank's two annual, two-week DR production exercises.

  • Compare dedicated QKD at selected data centres with conventional post-quantum VPN upgrades at branches, without claiming unmeasured performance, cost savings or regulatory compliance.

Cryptographic protection boundary and integration model¶

The bank's storage and application replication flows remain conventional data traffic. In the proposed architecture, supported encryption gateways or compatible application/transport security endpoints apply authenticated symmetric encryption, for example a correctly configured AES-256-based authenticated mode. A key-management layer identifies authorized endpoint pairs, handles key identifiers, rotation, access control, expiry, monitoring and audit, and transfers key material to the compatible encryption endpoints. I do not assume that IBM i, Oracle, IBM storage controllers or existing VPN appliances natively ingest QKD keys; those interfaces and vendors must be examined before a proof of concept.

Where compatible QKD equipment exists, separate optical quantum links could supply key material to authorized key-management entities. The original report identifies ETSI GS QKD 014 as a possible application-interface reference; actual device conformance and integration would need to be demonstrated. Existing fiber or IP paths would continue carrying encrypted replication traffic. A carrier data service does not automatically expose a transparent optical path suitable for QKD, and a public-internet VPN is not a quantum optical channel.

QKD-derived key material and a supported post-quantum key-establishment result could be incorporated under an explicitly validated hybrid key-derivation and authentication design. Merely concatenating keys or claiming a fallback is quantum-safe without a security analysis would be insufficient. Key generation, key usage and rekey intervals must be sized using measured application traffic, equipment limits and outage requirements. The present simulation does not demonstrate full-rate one-time-pad encryption, production replication throughput or an approved hybrid cryptographic protocol.

Conclusion and Future Work¶

This project developed a reproducible DPS-QKD simulation framework that connects protocol-level concepts to free-space optical propagation, detector behavior, atmospheric fading, machine-learning-assisted design search and trusted satellite extensions. The terrestrial 20 km study provides the baseline link budget; the turbulence model demonstrates state-by-state nonlinear evaluation and detection-weighted error pooling; the ML surrogate accelerates design exploration while retaining a physics-model recheck; and the satellite and banking sections show how the same framework can be extended to wider-area key-distribution architectures. All reported key-rate quantities remain engineering proxies under the stated assumptions rather than certified finite-key security bounds.

Answers to the Research Questions¶

1. Parameter sensitivity: within the model, increasing receiver aperture and interferometer visibility improves useful detection performance, while greater distance, background probability and deep fading generally reduce available signal and can increase QBER. Mean photon number has a trade-off: values that are too small reduce useful detections, whereas larger values increase the multiphoton fraction.

2. Order of averaging: a fluctuating channel should not in general be replaced by one average transmission value before nonlinear detector and entropy calculations. The report therefore evaluates each fading state first and pools detection/error statistics afterward. At the present weak-to-moderate baseline the pooled and mean-conditional rate proxies are close, but this numerical closeness is scenario-specific rather than a general equivalence.

3. ML-assisted search: the RBF kernel-ridge surrogate reproduces the simulated five-parameter performance surface with high held-out agreement inside the sampled domain and proposes an operating point close to the direct finite-grid optimum. The physics-model recheck remains authoritative, and deployment would additionally require measured channel and device data.

Future Work¶

Future work should extend the project from engineering proxies toward implementation-matched security analysis and experimental validation. Priorities include finite-key DPS security bounds, a full iterative LDPC reconciliation implementation with explicit leakage/verification accounting, measured Bahrain atmospheric and daylight-background data, detector afterpulsing/dead-time characterization, pointing-acquisition-tracking dynamics, real satellite ephemerides and weather availability, key-inventory scheduling across trusted relays, and a hardware proof of concept that tests vendor-supported KMS/HSM and QKD/PQC integration interfaces.
